How the brain finds the right paragraph

You ask "should I raise my prices?" and the strategist answers with a specific source citation in under a second. There are 9,558 paragraphs of Hormozi material sitting on your machine. How does it find the right ones, every time? Plain English, no PhD required.

01 · the problem

9,558 paragraphs. 200 milliseconds.

Everything Hormozi has published in 3 books and 2,275 videos lives on your laptop as text. When you ask the strategist a question, it needs to find the 5 most relevant paragraphs out of all of those, fast.

Keyword search is too dumb: it would miss "raise prices" when the source says "charge more." Reading all 9,558 paragraphs every time is too slow. We need something in the middle: fast and meaning-aware.

02 · the trick

Every paragraph gets a fingerprint

The trick is: every paragraph is converted into a numerical fingerprint. A list of 384 numbers. Paragraphs about similar things end up with similar fingerprints. The fingerprints capture the meaning, not the words.

"Raise prices on existing customers. Grandfather them for one cycle. Don't apologize, don't justify."
→
[ 0.41, -0.22, 0.78, 0.13, -0.55, … 379 more numbers … 0.91, -0.04 ]
"The fastest credible service in a market wins price. Charge heinous amounts."
→
[ 0.38, -0.19, 0.81, 0.16, -0.51, … 379 more numbers … 0.89, -0.07 ]
"Onboarding is the highest-leverage retention activity. Deliver one visible win in week one."
→
[ -0.62, 0.34, 0.05, -0.71, 0.42, … 379 more numbers … -0.18, 0.66 ]

Notice: the two pricing paragraphs (top two) have nearly identical numbers. The retention paragraph (bottom) has totally different numbers. The fingerprints know what each paragraph is about, even though the words are different.

03 · the model

Where the fingerprints come from

A small open-source AI model lives on your machine. About 80 MB on disk. Its only job: take a piece of text in, give 384 numbers out. Same input always produces the same output.

input Any piece of text (a paragraph, a sentence, a question)
↓
model: BGE-small-en-v1.5 (~80 MB on disk, runs on your CPU) Read the text, compute its 384-number fingerprint
↓
output [ 384 numbers describing the meaning ]

At setup time (one-time, ~20 minutes), this model processes all 9,558 paragraphs once. The 9,558 fingerprints get stacked into a single 13 MB file: brain/index.npz. That's the brain.

After setup, the model only ever runs on your future questions, not on the corpus again. Querying is fast because the heavy work is already done.

04 · the query

Your question gets a fingerprint too

When you ask the strategist a question, it runs the SAME model on your question. Your question becomes a 384-number fingerprint in the same number-space as the paragraphs.

"Should I raise my prices on existing customers?"
→
[ 0.40, -0.21, 0.79, 0.15, -0.53, … 379 more numbers … 0.90, -0.05 ]

Compare those numbers to the pricing paragraph fingerprints from step 02. They're almost identical. That's the system saying "this question is about the same kind of thing as those paragraphs."

Now we have:

  • 1 fingerprint for your question
  • 9,558 fingerprints for the paragraphs in the brain

The only thing left to do is find the closest paragraph fingerprints to your question's fingerprint.

05 · the search

Find the closest fingerprints

The math: how close are two fingerprints? Close means "same kind of meaning." Far means "different topic." For each of the 9,558 paragraph fingerprints, we compute a closeness score to your question. Sort. Take the top 5.

your question pricing (closest) leads (far) retention (far) scaling (far)

The actual fingerprint space has 384 dimensions, not 2. But the principle is the same: paragraphs about similar topics cluster together. Your question lands somewhere in this space, and the closest paragraphs win.

The closeness score per paragraph is one matrix multiplication. The whole comparison across 9,558 paragraphs takes under a millisecond.

06 · bonus pass

Keyword search runs at the same time

Fingerprint matching is smart but slightly blurry. When your question contains a specific phrase like "CLOSER framework" or "Rule of 100" or "Grand Slam Offer," exact-word matching catches things that fingerprints sometimes blur. So we run BOTH searches in parallel and combine.

The fingerprint pass

Your question becomes a 384-number fingerprint. Find paragraphs with similar fingerprints. Smart about meaning.

Catches: "should I price higher?" finds paragraphs about charging more, even though the words differ.

The keyword pass

Old-school search: which paragraphs contain the most relevant rare words from your question? Smart about exact terminology.

Catches: "explain the CLOSER framework" finds paragraphs that contain the word "CLOSER" verbatim.

Each pass produces its own top-30 list of paragraphs. We combine both lists with a simple formula (Reciprocal Rank Fusion): paragraphs that rank well in BOTH lists bubble to the top. Best of both worlds.

07 · diversity

Make sure the top 5 are different angles

If 5 of the top paragraphs all say roughly the same thing, that's a waste. So before returning the final 5, we pick for diversity: each new pick should be relevant AND different from the ones already picked. The strategist sees 5 different angles instead of 5 repeats.

08 · synthesis

The strategist takes over

Top 5 paragraphs handed to Claude. Claude reads them, applies them to your specific business (using profile.yaml), and answers in one paragraph. Cites the source by name. That's the response you see.

your question Should I raise my prices on existing customers?
↓
brain returns top 5 relevant paragraphs [pricing paragraph 1] [pricing paragraph 2] [video chunk] [bonus chunk] [$100M Offers chunk]
↓
claude reads them + your profile.yaml Applies the methodology to your specific revenue, price range, customer base.
↓
answer to you "Yes. Raise to $8,000. Per $100M Offers: grandfather existing customers for one cycle. Email Monday, 30 days notice. Don't apologize."

The strategist never makes things up from training data alone. Every claim is grounded in a paragraph from the brain. If the brain doesn't contain something, the strategist says so rather than guessing.

09 · the technical names

For the curious

You do not need to know any of these terms to use the system. But if you read a blog post about "RAG" or "embeddings" or "cosine similarity," now you know what they mean:

// what we called it: → // what it's actually called:

fingerprint             → "embedding" (a vector of 384 floats)
fingerprint generator   → "embedding model" (BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5)
distance score         → "cosine similarity"
keyword pass          → "BM25" (a 50-year-old algorithm, still excellent)
combining both lists    → "reciprocal rank fusion"
diversity filter       → "maximal marginal relevance (MMR)"
the whole pattern     → "retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)"
building the index     → "ingestion" (one-time, ~20 minutes)
10 · in one sentence

The whole thing, in a sentence

Convert every paragraph in the corpus into a list of 384 numbers that captures its meaning, do the same to your question, find the paragraphs whose numbers are closest, hand them to Claude, get an answer.

That's it. Everything else is a refinement: keyword search to catch exact phrases, diversity filters to avoid repetition, a profile.yaml so the answer is specific to your business. The core idea is just numbers that capture meaning.
